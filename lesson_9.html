Lesson 9: Main Courses and Dietary Requirement Management

The Main Course: Centerpiece of the Dining Experience

The main course is the centerpiece of the dining experience—the moment the entire meal has been building toward and the dish that will define the guest's culinary memory of the evening. It is at this juncture that the server's descriptive ability is tested most critically, because the main course is typically the most complex composition on the plate: multiple protein and vegetable elements, a sauce of elaborate preparation, a specific and intentional cooking technique, and a garnish that contributes both aesthetically and gastronomically.

Describing main courses with accuracy and passion requires a genuine understanding of the kitchen's philosophy and techniques. A server who cannot articulate the difference between a 'pan-seared' and 'oven-roasted' preparation, or who cannot explain what 'medium-rare' or 'blushing pink' means in precise terms, has failed in their fundamental role as the kitchen's ambassador to the table. Great main course descriptions are specific, sensory, and sequential—guiding the guest from the visual presentation to the primary flavor, then to the secondary elements, and finally to the overall dining experience the dish provides.

Dietary restriction management at the main course stage is a complex communications and logistical challenge. Allergen information must be 100% accurate: a mistake is not merely a service failure but potentially a medical emergency. The language used to manage dietary requirements must balance the guest's need for certainty with appropriate levels of professional qualification. Saying 'this dish is gluten-free' with absolute certainty is only appropriate when your kitchen has confirmed this through its cross-contamination protocols—otherwise 'this dish does not contain gluten as an ingredient, but if you have a serious intolerance, I would like to check with our kitchen directly' is the professionally and ethically correct response.

Professional Dialogue: Navigating Allergens and Dietary Needs

Teacher: "Today we address one of the most high-stakes communication scenarios in professional dining: the handling of serious food allergies. This is not merely a service issue—it is a safety issue. Let's work through a scenario. A guest tells you at the beginning of the meal that they have a severe tree nut allergy. What do you do?"
"I'd take that information very seriously immediately. I'd tell them: 'Thank you for letting me know—that's very important and I want to make sure your entire meal is completely safe tonight. Let me speak with our chef right now to confirm which dishes are completely safe for you and which may have cross-contamination risks.' And then I'd genuinely go and do that."
Teacher: "Excellent. Two critical moves there: firstly, you thanked the guest for sharing, which acknowledges that sharing an allergy can feel like an imposition and reassures them that it is welcome information. Secondly, you committed to verification from the kitchen—not from memory, not from the menu card, but from the chef. This is the only acceptable standard for serious allergen management. Now, the chef confirms that three of our main courses are safe and two contain almonds in their sauce. How do you communicate this back to the guest?"
"I'd return promptly and say: 'I've spoken with our chef, and I'm happy to confirm that our Côte de Boeuf, our Sea Bass, and our Mushroom Risotto are completely free of tree nuts and prepared in an area of the kitchen away from any nut ingredients. Our Duck and our Chicken, however, both have an almond-based sauce this evening, so I would steer you away from those two tonight.' Then I'd give the full description of the safe dishes to help them choose."
Teacher: "Perfect execution. Specific, clear, honest, and immediately followed by enthusiastic descriptions of the safe options—you haven't left the guest with a list of restrictions; you've redirected them to three excellent dishes with genuine enthusiasm. And the phrase 'I would steer you away from those two tonight' is professionally elegant—you're giving advice, not issuing a warning, and the word 'tonight' subtly implies the menu changes and invites them to return when those dishes might be prepared differently."

Material: The Fourteen Allergens — Professional Knowledge

The EU's Food Information for Consumers Regulation (FIC) and similar legislation in jurisdictions worldwide mandates that hospitality operations identify and clearly communicate the presence of fourteen major allergens in food and beverages. Every hospitality professional who interacts with food and guests is legally and ethically required to have working knowledge of these allergens and to be able to accurately communicate their presence or potential cross-contamination risk in every dish on the menu.

The fourteen major allergens are: Celery, Cereals containing gluten (wheat, barley, rye, oats), Crustaceans (prawns, crab, lobster), Eggs, Fish, Lupin, Milk, Molluscs (oysters, mussels, squid), Mustard, Nuts (almonds, cashews, walnuts, pistachios), Peanuts, Sesame seeds, Soybeans, and Sulphur dioxide and sulphites. A professional who can recite and explain these allergens in English is an asset to any establishment operating in an international environment.

Vocabulary Focus

AllergenA substance causing an allergic reaction in sensitive individuals. In food service, the fourteen legislatively defined major allergens must be declared on menus and communicated to guests upon request.
Cross-ContaminationThe transfer of allergen particles from one food item to another through shared surfaces, utensils, or hands. Even a dish with no allergen ingredients can be unsafe if cross-contamination has occurred.
Vegan vs. Plant-basedVegan excludes all animal products (including honey, dairy, and eggs). Plant-based primarily refers to food composition without necessarily excluding all animal-derived ingredients.
Cooking TemperatureThe internal temperature to which protein is cooked: Rare (52°C/125°F), Medium-Rare (57°C/135°F), Medium (63°C/145°F), Medium-Well (68°C/155°F), Well Done (74°C/165°F).
