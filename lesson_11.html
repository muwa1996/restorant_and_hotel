Lesson 11: Wine Selection and Sommelier Service

The Language of Wine: From List to Glass

Wine service is one of the most linguistically sophisticated areas of professional hospitality. It requires a fusion of scientific knowledge (grape varieties, viticulture, fermentation), geographic literacy (wine regions, appellations, terroir), sensory vocabulary (aroma descriptors, tannin structure, acidity), and a sophisticated ability to translate this expertise into accessible, enthusiastic recommendations for guests with vastly different levels of wine knowledge. The professional who masters wine language in English holds a key that opens doors to the highest levels of restaurant service worldwide.

The sommelier—or wine-trained server—plays a role that is part educator, part storyteller, and part psychologist. Their job is to assess each guest's relationship with wine through a few carefully chosen questions, identify the ideal selection from the restaurant's list that will match both the food they have ordered and the flavor profile they prefer, and present that recommendation in a way that is enticing, credible, and respectful of whatever level of wine expertise the guest brings to the table. This last point is critical: wine language should never be a performance of knowledge that intimidates; it should always be a sharing of knowledge that delights.

Professional Dialogue: Navigating the Wine List with a Guest

Teacher: "Good evening. Tonight we practice the wine consultation—possibly the most nuanced conversation in fine dining service. A table of two has ordered the tasting menu. Before discussing specific bottles, how do you open the wine conversation?"
"I'd begin with a broad, open question that invites them into the conversation rather than immediately presenting them with a list: 'Would you like to explore the wine pairing for the tasting menu this evening, or would you prefer to select a bottle or bottles from our wine list?'"
Teacher: "Excellent opening. You've presented two valid options rather than assuming, which shows respect for every preference. They say they'd like to choose a bottle and ask for your recommendation with the first four courses, which include seafood and poultry. What do you ask next?"
"I'd ask about their general taste preferences: 'Do you tend to enjoy white wine, or do you prefer something with a little lower acidity? Are you drawn to richer, fuller wines or something more elegant and restrained?' And I'd also feel the room for budget—though I'd never ask directly. I might say: 'I have some wonderful options at various price points. Is there a particular style or region you're fond of?'"
Teacher: "The budget question handled with beautiful indirectness—you've opened the door for them to signal preference without the awkwardness of a direct price discussion. They say they enjoy white Burgundy. How do you respond?"
"I'd show genuine enthusiasm and narrow quickly: 'Wonderful choice for your menu tonight. A white Burgundy—Chardonnay from Burgundy—is going to be a magnificent match. We have a Meursault from Domaine Leflaive on our list, and also a lovely Pouilly-Fuissé that's slightly more approachable in style. The Meursault has a gorgeous richness and a nutty complexity that I think will complement both your seafood and the poultry beautifully.'"
Teacher: "You've given them two real options with clear differentiation, used the word 'magnificent match' to anchor the quality of the recommendation, and described the wine using sensory language they can actually engage with—'richness, nutty complexity.' This is sommelier-level service language. You've earned your guest's trust."

Material: Wine Tasting Vocabulary — The Language of Sensory Description

Describing wine requires a specific vocabulary that bridges the sensory and the intellectual. The great wine writers—Hugh Johnson, Jancis Robinson, Oz Clarke—demonstrate that wine description can be both highly technical and deeply poetic. For the restaurant professional, the goal is not literary wine writing but clear, evocative, and memorable descriptions that communicate the wine's character and create desire in the listener.

Wine is typically described through four key sensory dimensions. Aroma: the primary smells (fruit, floral, herbal) and secondary/tertiary developments (vanilla from oak, earthy minerality, toast, leather, beeswax). Palate: the immediate impression of sweetness or dryness, followed by the perception of acidity (which makes your mouth water), tannin (which creates a drying sensation on the gums), alcohol (which provides warmth), and body (is it light as water or as full as cream?). Finish: the length of time the flavors persist after swallowing—a great wine has a finish measured in seconds, not moments. Balance: whether these four elements (fruit, acid, tannin, alcohol) exist in harmonious relationship or whether one overwhelms the others. A wine described as 'balanced' signals confident quality to any experienced diner.

Vocabulary Focus

TerroirThe complete environmental context—soil, climate, altitude, orientation to sun—that gives a wine its unique character. The concept of terroir explains why wines from the same grape taste different depending on where they are grown.
AppellationA legally defined geographic area used to identify the origin of wine. French appellations (e.g., Bordeaux, Champagne) carry strict production rules that guarantee specific quality parameters.
VintageThe year in which the grapes used to make a wine were harvested. Vintage variation—which is significant in classic wine regions—is an important quality indicator communicated to knowledgeable guests.
DecantingThe process of pouring wine from its bottle into a glass vessel to allow it to breathe and separate from any sediment. Typically done with mature red wines and recommended as both a practical and theatrical service gesture.
